\section{Introduction}


The effective population size of a population, $N_e$, is a central parameter in evolutionary biology and population genetics. There is a long tradition of studying temporal changes in $N_e$ over large time scales to understand the evolutionary history of a population. More recently, $N_e$ has been proposed as a key indicator in conservation biology by different organisations \citep{van_Oosterhout_2026}. A reduced $N_e$ decreases the efficiency of natural selection and increases the strength of genetic drift. As a consequence, estimating $N_e$ is a crucial component to predict a population's ability to persist and thrive in the Anthropocene.

In an applied conservation context, we are interested in estimating $N_e$ for very recent generations. However, obtaining reliable estimates of very recent $N_e$ (say, $< 100$ generations in the past) is challenging. For example, estimates based solely on genetic diversity (also known as sample heterozygosity), although widely used, are highly limited for this application as they are largely shaped by the $N_e$ of the ancestral population many thousands of generations in the past \citep{van_Oosterhout_2026}. Other popular methods like PSMC \citep{Liu_2016}, MSMC2 \citep{Schiffels_2020} or Bayesian Skyline Plots \citep{Drummond_2005} also provide limited resolution at these time scales \citep{van_Oosterhout_2026}.

Generally, we expect methods that depend on site-summary statistics to struggle resolving recent demography as they depend on mutations, which accumulate slowly. As a result, there is often a pronounced delay, or time-lag \citep{Gargiulo_2025}, between the time of a major demographic disturbance and the time when we can observe notable changes in site statistics (see later XXX).

Alternatively, we may use the signal of recombination events during meiosis. Because recombination events happen much more frequently, they provide a more informative signal to study very recent $N_e$. One could then expect methods that (approximately) model genome sequences under the coalescence with recombination (such as PSMC or MSMC2) to infer accurately recent (as well as ancient) demography. In this case, however, they are limited by the sample sizes for which inference remains tractable. Recent pairwise coalescence events of a handful of chromosomes are rare, which therefore imposes limits on the time-scale resolution of these methods.

Provided that one has access to a genetic map, alternative summary statistics of genomic variation have been proven successful. Namely, identity-by-descent sharing (IBDNe \citep{Browning_2015}, HapNe-IBD \citep{Fournier_2023}) and linkage disequilibrium (GONE \citep{Santiago_2020}, HapNe-LD \citep{Fournier_2023}). These methods target these summary statistics because they are almost exclusively shaped by recent recombination events, which makes them an interesting target of inference. However, they are not informative of ancient demography.

In this work, we propose a model-based Bayesian inference framework for inferring recent $N_e(t)$ from unphased genomic data. We make two major contributions. First, we investigate to what extent we can exploit the proxy between recent shared ancestry and long-range linkage disequilibrium proposed by \citet{Fournier_2023} outside non-human populations. Second, we introduce a novel inference method that jointly models mean sample heterozygosity and linkage disequilibrium decay to fit parametric demographic models, combining analytical and Monte Carlo predictions.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Choice of informative summary statistics}

The genetic diversity, $\pi$ is a classical genetic summary statistic. It's expected value, $\mathbb{E}[\pi]$, is defined as the probability that a pair of randomly chosen chromosomes differ at a given nucleotide site. It is dominated by the $N_e$ of the ancestral population many thousands of generations ago. As a result, it is weakly informative of recent $N_e$, and we must wait a substantial amount of time in order to observe appreciable changes in $\mathbb{E}[\pi]$. This is an alternative interpretation of a phenomenon known as genetic drift debt \citep{Gargiulo_2025}.

On the contrary, measures of linkage disequilibrium are more sensitive to recent changes in $N_e$ and are a more powerful target for inference of recent demographic history. Let $G_{x,i}$ denote the genotype (allele count, $\{0, 1\}$ if haploid or $\{0, 1, 2\}$ if diploid) at locus $x$ in individual $i$. Here, we focus on the product of centered and standardized genotypes at two bi-allelic loci

\begin{equation}
	X_i X_j Y_i Y_j,
	\quad \text{where loci } x \text{ and } y \text{ are } u \text{ Morgan apart},
\end{equation}

where $X_i$ and $Y_i$ corresponds to $G_{x,i}$ and $G_{y,i}$ transformed such the resulting random variable has mean zero and unit variance (see Appendix XXX).

Empirically, we can estimate it by averaging across several pairs of individuals and across many pairs of loci $x, y$ whose distance falls in a given bin $u\in[l, r]$. We denote such an estimator as $\overline{X_i X_j Y_i Y_j}$ and, because of the law of large numbers (see Appendix XXX), it converges to $\mathbb E [	X_i X_j Y_i Y_j]$ as the number of pairs of loci and individuals grows.

\subsection{Case study: a very recent catastrophic disturbance}

To illustrate how these summary statistics are informative at different temporal scales, we study the impact of a strong recent bottleneck in a population. Consider an instantaneous bottleneck that happened as recently as five generations ago that results in a tenfold decrease in current $Ne$ (Figure \ref{fig:conceptual:demography}). In a conservation setting, this model mimics the effect of some catastrophic disturbance.

Compared to some reference values (a population in which such bottlenecks did not occur), we predict a decrease in genetic diversity. However, the observed simulated loss in diversity (Figure \ref{fig:conceptual:pi_hist}) is relatively very small and would be difficult to detect empirically. Likewise, we predict an increase in $\mathbb E[X_iX_jY_iY_j]$ (as well as other measures of linkage disequilibrium) because in a smaller population, we expect fewer recombination events to break apart the ancestral haplotypes shared by any pair of chromosomes.

$\overline{X_iX_jY_iY_j}$ is a more informative summary statistic in the sense that we expect a higher relative change (Figure \ref{fig:conceptual:ld}) between both scenarios. In practice, we measure $\overline{X_iX_jY_iY_j}$ across pairs of loci at different binning distances. As genetic distance increases, $\mathbb{E}[X_iX_jY_iY_j]$ must decrease as there are more opportunities for recombination to occur between them. The rate at which $\mathbb{E}[X_iX_jY_iY_j]$ is, itself, informative of recent demography.

\subsection{Analytical predictions}

Predictions of informative summary statistics are what allow us to confront demographic models with genomic data. Since full-genome probabilistic models are intractable, we rely on the derived expectations $\mathbb E[\pi]$ and $\mathbb E[X_iX_jY_iY_j]$, which we then compare against mean values estimated over large windows across thousands of loci. Throughout, we consider a panmictic population whose effective size follows a parametric trajectory $N_e(t \mid \theta)$ (e.g.\ constant, piecewise-constant, or piecewise-exponential).

In coalescence of coalescence theory, $N_e$ can be defined in terms of the inverse of the instantaneous coalescence rate, that we denote as $\lambda(t)$. For a diploid population:

\begin{equation}
	\lambda(t \mid \theta) = \frac{1}{2N_e(t \mid \theta)}
\end{equation}

It is well known that, for a panmictic population of constant size $N$, $\mathbb{E}[\pi] = 4\mu N$, where $\mu$ is the mutation rate XXX. More generally,

\begin{equation}
	\mathbb{E}[\pi \mid \theta] = 2\mu \int_0^\infty t\, f(t \mid \theta)\, dt,
	\label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}

where $f(t \mid \theta)$ is the coalescence density (i.e.\ the probability density of two lineages finding their most recent common ancestor $t$ generations ago). At any given time, this density factorises into the probability of not having yet coalesced times the instantaneous coalescence rate so that

\begin{equation}
	f(t \mid \theta) = \lambda(t \mid \theta)\, \exp\!\left(-\int_0^t \lambda(s \mid \theta)\, ds\right).
	\label{eq:tmrca}
\end{equation}

Unbiased predictions of $\mathbb{E}[\pi \mid \theta]$ can be obtained by solving Equation \ref{eq:tmrca} for different forms of $N_e(t)$ trajectories and plugging them back in Equation \ref{eq:pi}. From Equation \ref{eq:pi} it is also apparent why the $\pi$ is weakly informative of recent $N_e$, as it's value will most likely dominated by the long tail in the integral.

Unfortunately, no analogous closed form is known for $\mathbb E[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta]$. Nevertheless, one can calculate an unbiased Montecarlo estimator by averaging over $M$ independent coalescent simulations under the demographic model $N_e(t \mid \theta)$. In each of $M$ replicates we simulate a pair of loci $u$ Morgans apart in a sample of $n$ individuals.
\begin{equation}
	\widehat{\mathbb E}_{\mathrm{MC}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta] = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \binom{n}{2}^{-1} \sum_{i<j} X_i^{(m)} X_j^{(m)} Y_i^{(m)} Y_j^{(m)},
	\label{eq:mc_ld}
\end{equation}
where $X_i^{(m)}, Y_i^{(m)}$ are the centred and standardised genotypes of individual $i$ at the two loci in replicate $m$.

Although unbiased, the Montecarlo estimator is computationally expensive. \citet{Fournier_2023} found the approximation expression and made an elegant link between long-range linkage-disequilibrium and coalescence with recombination. They suggested that pairs of high-frequency loci that are not very close ($u > 0.5 cM$)  satisfy

\begin{equation}
	\mathbb E[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta] \approx \widehat{\mathbb E}_{\mathrm{F}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta] =  S(u \mid \theta),
	\label{eq:fournier_ld}
\end{equation}

where $S(u)$ is the probability that in pair of randomly chosen chromosomes two loci that are $u$ Morgans apart are in identity-by-descent (i.e. they have not been broken apart by recombination since their most recent common ancestor (MRCA)). They obtained identical approximations for the haploid and diploid case, as well as a correction factor that improve predictions with finite sample size in diploids (see Appendix XXX, it doens't work in haploids).

Under the Sequentially Markovian coalescent approximation, one can further simplify Equation \ref{eq:fournier_ld} and to obtain

\begin{equation}
	\widehat{\mathbb E}_{\mathrm{F}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta] \stackrel{\text{by SMC}}{\approx} \int_0^\infty \exp(-2tu)\, f(t \mid \theta)\, dt
	\label{eq:smc}
\end{equation}

that is, the probability that no recombination event has happened since their MRCA.

Comparing Equation \ref{eq:smc} and Equation \ref{eq:pi} provides insight on why $\mathbb E[X_iX_jY_iY_j]$ is a more informative summary statistic of recent demography than $\mathbb E[\pi]$. A possible interpretation is that Equation \ref{eq:pi} assigns weights that increase linearly with time whereas in Equation \ref{eq:smc} decay exponentially with time. Both expressions depend on the pairwise coalescence density $f(t \mid \theta)$ which is often very small in recent generations (small values of $t$). However, because of the "re-weighting" we observe that $\mathbb E[X_iX_jY_iY_j]$ is much more sensitive to $N_e$ in very recent generations.

\subsection{Prediction error}

The estimator proposed by \citet{Fournier_2023} is surprisingly elegant. However, their application seemed to be limited to human populations. Next, we evaluate the bias introduced by the approximation in a broader range of species and demographic models.

First, we simulated full-genome genomic variation data for different species models in the stdpopsim's catalogue \citep{Lauterbur_2023}. The goal was to test the predictions in realistic parameters regime. Results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:stdpopsim}. Overall, we found $\widehat{\mathbb E}_{\mathrm{F}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j]$ to match well simulated summary statistic across various sampling configurations, demographic histories and recombination rates ($10^{-11}$ to $10^{-8}$ per base pair per generation). \subsection{Inference scheme}

Here, we introduce a Bayesian inference scheme that combines analytical predictions of sample heterozygosity ($\pi$) and $X_iX_jY_iY_j$ with Monte Carlo predictions. Our approach might be seen as a hybrid between simulation-based inference (or approximate Bayesian computation) and exact probabilistic inference. The key idea is to use a surrogate likelihood based on analytical predictions and a handful of Monte Carlo simulations to correct their bias.

First, we consider a set of $N_D$ large genomic windows, which we assume to be independent and identically distributed (an assumption that could be relaxed). For each window $w$, we compute the mean sample heterozygosity $\hat\pi$ as well as $\overline{X_iX_jY_iY_j}$ across pairs of loci whose distances fall in $P$ bins of increasing size (e.g. $b_1=[0.05, 0.1]$ Morgan). We collect the $P+1$ summary statistics of window $w$ into a single vector $S_w = (S_{w,0}, S_{w,1}, \dots, S_{w,P})$, where $S_{w,0} = \hat\pi$ and $S_{w,b} = \overline{X_iX_jY_iY_j}$ in bin $b$. By the (multivariate) central limit theorem, if each window contains many loci (as it typically does), this vector approximately follows a multivariate normal distribution.

The natural Bayesian model to do inference on is therefore

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\theta         & \sim \text{demographic prior}                              \\
		\mu(\theta)    & = \mathbb{E}[S \mid \theta], \quad
		\mu_0 = \mathbb{E}[\pi \mid \theta], \quad
		\mu_b = \mathbb{E}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta] \ \text{in bin } b             \\
		\Sigma(\theta) & = \mathrm{Cov}(S \mid \theta)                              \\
		S_w            & \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mu(\theta),\, \Sigma(\theta)\big),
		\quad w = 1, \dots, N_D.
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:natural_model}
\end{equation}

Here, $\mu(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[S \mid \theta]$ collects the expectations of each summary statistic, $\mathbb{E}[S_{w,b} \mid \theta]$ and only its first entry, $\mathbb{E}[\pi \mid \theta]$, is known analytically. The covariance $\Sigma(\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}(S \mid \theta)$ is unknown, and depends not only on $\theta$ but also on parameters such as the sample size and the mutation rate.

In order to make inference tractable, one could do approximate inference by plugging in either the Monte Carlo estimator $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathrm{MC}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta]$ (which is computationally expensive) or the closed-form estimator $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathrm{F}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta]$ (which is biased). Rather than deciding between one or the other, we propose to augment the empirical dataset with a set of synthetic data points from which we jointly learn the bias of $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathrm{F}}$. In particular, we model the relative bias (the relative difference between the closed-form estimator and the unbiased Monte Carlo estimator) as a Gaussian process regression.

Gaussian processes (GP) are a popular choice for non-parametric regression, as they provide a probability distribution over functions and generalize to non-linear functions. We construct a synthetic dataset by evaluating the Monte Carlo estimator at $N_S$ different parameter values $\tilde\theta_1, \dots, \tilde\theta_{N_S}$. For each, we obtain the relative bias of the closed-form estimator and its associated Monte Carlo error. We then fit one GP per bin. For simplicity, we project $\tilde\theta$ into an appropriate one-dimensional summary when training the GP. We found that $\log\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathrm{F}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \theta]$ is an informative summary and works well in practice, although different approximations (such as neural networks) could be used. For each bin $b$ we fit:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\phi    & \sim \text{GP hyperprior}                                   \\
		g_b     & \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(0,\, k_\phi\big)                      \\
		r_{i,b} & \sim \mathcal{N}\big(g_b(z_{i,b}),\, \epsilon_{i,b}^2\big),
		\quad i = 1, \dots, N_S,
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:gp_bias}
\end{equation}

where $z_{i,b} = \log\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathrm{F}}[X_iX_jY_iY_j \mid \tilde\theta_i]$ in bin $b$, $r_{i,b}$ is the relative bias at that point, and $\epsilon_{i,b}$ is its Monte Carlo standard error. In order to speed up inference, we approximate the GP with a Hilbert space basis function expansion \citep{XXX_hsgp}.

The choice of synthetic data points determines in which region of parameter space the GP learns the bias, which is not known a priori. To avoid spending computational resources learning the bias in regions that are incompatible with the empirical data, we propose an active-learning procedure where we augment the synthetic dataset with candidate $\tilde\theta$ drawn from the model fitted in the previous round. We parametrise the GP with weakly informative priors centred at zero, so that in regions not covered by the synthetic data points we do not assume the bias is positive or negative.

We have not yet discussed how to estimate $\Sigma(\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}(S \mid \theta)$, for which we have no analytical prediction. Note that its form is not trivial. It depends on the parameters (it is heteroscedastic), and we expect it to show strong correlations between dimensions (consider, for example, two adjacent bins). As a pragmatic choice, we set a data-informed prior and infer $\Sigma$ jointly with the rest of the parameters. Accurately inferring a high-dimensional covariance matrix requires a large amount of data, which may exceed the number of empirical observations. Without further assumptions, we can combine the empirical data with the Monte Carlo estimates to obtain better estimates. In addition, we assume that $\Sigma$ is locally approximately constant, and use the coalescent simulations from the previous active-learning round to inform its estimation without increasing the computational cost.

In summary, the inference scheme we propose consists of a tractable surrogate likelihood that we sequentially improve in the region of interest through an active-learning procedure. The idea of using a Gaussian process was inspired by GPS-ABC \citep{gps_abc}. In addition to their original idea, we exploit the structure of our problem and the active-learning procedure to obtain a better approximation. The idea of active learning was inspired by sequential neural likelihood \citep{pmlr-v89-papamakarios19a}. Compared to traditional ABC, our approach benefits from gradients to infer higher-dimensional models and may require fewer simulations. Note also that the bias is highest when $N_e$ is low, a regime in which coalescent simulations are cheapest, so the parameter values that require the most correction are also the cheapest to simulate.

The resulting model has thousands of parameters, which we fit with the No-U-Turn Sampler \citep{nuts} as implemented in Stan \citep{XXX_stan}. During active learning, we instead rely on Pathfinder \citep{pathfinder} for fast approximate inference.


\subsection{Simulation-based calibration}

To validate the proposed inference scheme and the approximate surrogate likelihood, we performed extensive simulation-based calibration (SBC) experiments \citep{Talts_2020} across several demographic models, in parameter regimes of both high and low estimator bias. SBC exploits the self-consistency property of Bayesian models: if $\theta \sim p(\theta)$ and $\tilde S \sim p(S \mid \theta)$, then the posterior draws $\theta' \sim p(\theta \mid \tilde S)$ are marginally distributed according to the prior $p(\theta)$. A calibrated inference procedure must therefore produce posterior probability integral transform (PIT) values that are uniformly distributed.

Accordingly, we assess calibration by inspecting the uniformity of the PIT values of the posterior samples with respect to the prior samples. Figure~\ref{fig:sbc} shows the result for a piecewise-exponential demographic model in a regime of relatively high estimator bias. To measure the effectiveness of our bias-corrected scheme (Figure~\ref{fig:sbc:corrected}), we compare it against a more naive model (Figure~\ref{fig:sbc:approximate}) that makes no attempt to correct the bias and instead assumes the bins are independent under a data-calibrated prior. This naive model corresponds to the natural extension of the likelihood used by \citet{Fournier_2023} in their penalized-likelihood optimization.
